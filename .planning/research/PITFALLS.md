# Pitfalls Research

**Domain:** Public open-source AI agent skill for persuasive technical presales writing, anchored in trademarked commercial sales methodologies, shipping measured benchmarks (sibling project to SimpleEnglish/ASD-STE100)
**Researched:** 2026-09-10
**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (design/process pitfalls and LLM-eval pitfalls are HIGH confidence, backed by cited research; trademark specifics are MEDIUM confidence — sourced but explicitly flagged as not legal advice)

This file is written against the concrete decisions already logged in `.planning/PROJECT.md`: Command of the Message as rule spine, MEDDICC as completeness audit, Challenger for the opening rule, the deletion test as the buzzword rule, an Integrity section that refuses fabrication, a full eval harness in v1, and the SimpleEnglish trademark posture reused for three rights-holders instead of one.

## Critical Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: The skill produces STE-flavored prose that kills the persuasion it exists to enable

**What goes wrong:**
The rule catalog is built the same way SimpleEnglish's is — numbered, checkable, deletion-tested, allergic to hedges and unquantified superlatives. Every one of those mechanisms is also a way to flatten voice. Applied uncritically to presales writing, the output reads as compliance prose: correct, evidenced, and dead. Reviewers (and buyers) don't reject it for being wrong; they reject it for being unpersuasive in the specific way an evaluator can smell — no narrative tension, no sense that the author cares whether the deal closes, every sentence hedged into a fact-statement with the emotional temperature of a spec sheet. This is the project's central design risk, named explicitly in PROJECT.md's Core Value and Constraints sections, and it is the one failure mode that cannot be fixed later by adding more rules — more constraint is the disease, not the cure.

**Why it happens:**
Controlled-language and plain-language programs are optimized for *comprehension under high stakes* (a tired mechanic, a non-native reader, a person who cannot ask a follow-up question). Persuasive writing optimizes for a different objective: getting a skeptical, time-pressured evaluator to form a belief and act on it. The research on plain-language summarization is a live demonstration of the tension: even purpose-built plain-language interventions for medical/scientific writing under-deliver (only 2.8% of Cochrane plain-language summaries hit "plain English" readability despite an entire methodology aimed at it; median reading grade required was 14.9 years of education) — i.e., simplification rules are hard to get right even when persuasion is *not* a competing goal. Add a persuasion mandate on top of a rule catalog built for clarity, and the two objectives pull in opposite directions unless the rules are explicitly designed to preserve rhetorical structure (contrast, stakes, specificity-as-persuasion) rather than just banning bad words. SimpleEnglish resolves the tension by refusing the genre outright ("deletes persuasion by design," "flat on purpose," "Do not apply it to marketing copy"). This project cannot take that exit — persuasion is the deliverable — so it must solve the tension the parent project sidestepped.

**How to avoid:**
- Make the rule catalog's stated mechanism for persuasion explicit and separate from its mechanism for cleanliness. Command of the Message's Before/After scenario contrast, named metrics, and differentiators *are* the persuasion engine — specificity substituting for adjectives, not the absence of adjectives substituting for nothing. Every rule that removes something (buzzwords, unsupported claims, hedges) must be paired with a rule that requires something concrete to replace it (a number, a named capability, a contrast). A rule catalog that only subtracts will converge on SimpleEnglish's voice by default.
- Explicitly preserve rhetorical devices that are not slop: the Challenger-style reframe, contrast structures ("today X costs you Y; after Z, that becomes W"), second-person address to the buyer's stated priorities, and confident (not hedged) claims when the claim is evidenced. Do not let the linter or the self-check treat these as violations.
- Build a "flatness" pressure test into the pressure-test suite from day one (see Pitfall 6): give the skill a genuinely high-stakes, competitive scenario (a deal the customer is about to lose to a competitor) and grade the output not just for violations-per-100-words but for a persuasion rubric — does a domain expert reading it *want* to keep reading, does it create urgency, does it feel like it was written by someone who understands and wants this specific deal. A text that scores zero linter violations and reads like an ISO procedure has failed this test even though it "passed."
- Run the blind pairwise judge (already planned per PROJECT.md) with a rubric dimension explicitly labeled something like "persuasive force" or "conviction," scored independently from "clarity" and "evidence density," so a flat-but-clean draft cannot hide behind a high aggregate score.

**Warning signs:**
- Worked examples on the fictional cloud-migration deal read identically in tone regardless of which artifact type (executive summary vs. RFP answer) they belong to — persuasion should modulate by artifact, compliance shouldn't.
- Internal reviewers describe drafts as "correct" or "clean" but never "compelling" or "convincing."
- The self-check step only ever finds things to *delete* (buzzwords, hedges, unsupported claims) and never surfaces a prompt to *add* something (a metric, a contrast, a reframe).
- Blind pairwise judge scores rise on "no fabrication" and "clarity" dimensions but stay flat or drop on any dimension related to conviction/urgency/persuasiveness.

**Phase to address:**
Rule catalog authoring phase (the persuasion-preserving pairing must be designed into the rules from the first draft, not patched in later) and the pressure-test/benchmark phase (persuasion must be a scored dimension, not an afterthought).

### Pitfall 2: The deletion test breaks silently on connotation, customer-anchored terms, RFP-mandated wording, and half-empty compounds

**What goes wrong:**
The governing buzzword rule — a term is legal if deleting it changes the technical meaning — is elegant and maintenance-free, but it is a test for *denotation*, and several classes of real presales language carry their value in something other than denotation:
- **Connotation-only terms** ("enterprise-grade," "battle-tested," "mission-critical"): deleting them often does *not* change the literal technical claim, so the test correctly flags them as air — but these terms sometimes function as a signal the buyer's own procurement language expects, and blanket removal can read as under-selling relative to a competitor's proposal using the same vocabulary the RFP invited.
- **Customer-first terms**: when the customer's own RFP or discovery notes use a term ("resilient," "future-proof," "cloud-native"), deleting the term from the response changes the *rhetorical alignment* with the customer even when it doesn't change the technical claim. The deletion test, applied mechanically, will delete language the customer explicitly asked for, which reads as ignoring the brief.
- **RFP question-wording terms**: many RFPs literally ask "describe your solution's resiliency" or "explain your zero-trust approach" — the response is graded against the question's own vocabulary by a scoring rubric the vendor never sees. A skill that strips the RFP's own terms in the name of the deletion test can cause the response to *lose points on a compliance matrix* it cannot see, which is a business-harming false positive, not a stylistic nicety.
- **Compound terms where one half is real and one is air** ("next-generation zero trust," "best-of-breed landing zone," "seamless FinOps"): the deletion test as stated operates term-by-term, but marketing modifiers attached to a legitimate technical noun don't get caught by a whole-term deletion check unless the rule explicitly requires testing each token independently and disaggregating modifier from noun.

**Why it happens:**
The rule was designed (correctly) to solve the technical-noun-vs.-air problem for *the writer's own vocabulary*. It was not designed for terms whose legality depends on an external referent — what the customer said, what the RFP asked, what a scoring rubric rewards — because the deletion test only has access to the sentence in front of it, not the customer's document or the evaluator's rubric. A test that is purely internal to the sentence cannot represent facts that live outside the sentence.

**How to avoid:**
- Split the rule into two passes: (1) the deletion test as originally stated, applied per-token (not per-phrase) so a real noun does not launder a decorative modifier riding on it ("next-generation" gets stripped, "zero trust" survives); (2) a **provenance override**: if a flagged term appears verbatim in the customer's own RFP text or stated requirements, it is retained and marked with its source ("customer's term, retained for compliance-matrix alignment") rather than deleted — the skill should not silently rewrite around the evaluator's own scoring vocabulary.
- For RFP-mandated wording specifically, add an explicit rule: when responding to a scored question, mirror the question's key terms in the answer's topic sentence even if those terms would otherwise fail the deletion test, and follow immediately with the concrete evidence that gives the term denotation in this response ("Our zero-trust approach: every service-to-service call is authenticated via mTLS and authorized per-request against policy X — no term is legal without adjacent proof").
- For connotation-only terms with no customer/RFP anchor, keep the deletion test as the default and require the replacement to carry the connotation via a concrete claim instead ("enterprise-grade" → "used by three customers running >500 TB workloads," or, absent real evidence, deleted with no substitute — see Pitfall 7 on fabrication).
- Document this as a named edge-case list in the rule catalog itself (not just in this research file), the same way SimpleEnglish documents known ceiling cases for its linter — an undocumented exception looks like an inconsistently applied rule; a documented one looks like a considered design.

**Warning signs:**
- Worked examples that respond to a simulated RFP question strip the RFP's own vocabulary from the answer's topic sentence.
- The deletion test, run on a compound term, treats the whole phrase as one unit and returns a single verdict instead of a per-token one.
- Pressure-test scenarios that supply a customer email or RFP excerpt containing a "buzzword" are not in the pressure-test suite at all — meaning this edge case was never actually exercised before ship.

**Phase to address:**
Rule catalog authoring phase (define the per-token test and the provenance override as part of the rule, not as a caveat discovered later) and per-artifact patterns phase (RFP/RFI response patterns must include the mirror-the-question rule explicitly).

### Pitfall 3: Trademark exposure across three live, actively contested marks — including inter-vendor litigation over the exact acronym family named in this project

**What goes wrong:**
PROJECT.md commits to naming Command of the Message (Force Management), MEDDICC/MEDDIC (multiple training vendors), and Challenger (Challenger Inc./its trademark successors) by name, with a stated posture of "adopt the SimpleEnglish posture exactly." Three things make this riskier than the ASD-STE100 case SimpleEnglish is modeling:

1. **ASD-STE100 has one rights-holder and no rival claimants.** This project's anchor space has *three* rights-holders, and — critically — the MEDDIC/MEDDICC/MEDDPICC family has **active inter-vendor trademark litigation between commercial competitors over the acronym itself**. A federal court (E.D. Pa., Civil Action No. 24-1836, ruling reported April 2026) found the term "MEDDPICC" generic and ordered cancellation of a registration held by one vendor (01 Consulting LLC / Lahoutifard) after a suit brought by MEDDICC Ltd — the court noted Lahoutifard registered the term 16 years after its conception by someone else, and that the enforcement history included takedowns of LinkedIn posts, YouTube videos, and Amazon book listings, and social-media suspensions targeting MEDDICC Ltd and its customers. This tells us two things relevant to this project: (a) the exact acronym family has a documented history of one party using trademark enforcement against community content (posts, videos, books) that merely *discussed* the methodology, which is exactly the kind of content this skill's SKILL.md and README are; and (b) "MEDDPICC" specifically has now been ruled generic by a federal court, but "MEDDICC" and "MEDDIC" are separate marks/spellings held by other claimants and this ruling does not obviously resolve their status — treating the whole family as "safe because a court called one spelling generic" would be a misreading.
2. **Command of the Message and Challenger are, per public secondary sources, still actively asserted trademarks** of their respective owners (Force Management; Challenger Inc./CEB's successor rights), with no comparable genericness ruling found in this research. They should be treated as live, enforceable marks.
3. **Reproducing training material is the actual line, and it is easy to cross by accident** in an AI-generated rule catalog, because models trained on the public web have almost certainly ingested descriptions, summaries, and in some cases near-verbatim paraphrases of these training decks. A model asked to "explain Command of the Message's six components" can easily reproduce structure and phrasing close enough to the proprietary training material to raise a derivative-work concern, not just a trademark concern — this is a copyright risk layered on top of the trademark risk, and it is model-generated-content-specific in a way the SimpleEnglish case (grounded in a public standard, paraphrased by a human) was not.

**Why it happens:**
Open-source projects that name commercial methodologies by trademarked name, without a licensing or affiliation relationship, are legally relying on nominative fair use — a real, recognized defense, but one with hard boundaries: the three-part test requires (a) the referenced thing isn't otherwise identifiable without the mark, (b) no more of the mark is used than necessary to identify it, and (c) nothing suggests sponsorship or endorsement. Projects get this wrong by using logos, using the mark in the *project's own name or branding* (which reads as source confusion, not reference), overstating "based on" or "certified in" language, or — the sharper risk here — by having an LLM regenerate what reads like the vendor's own training content rather than an independent paraphrase of the publicly observable idea.

**How to avoid (not legal advice — flag for professional review before v1 ships):**
- **Never put a trademarked methodology name in the project name, tagline, badge, or logo.** "Proof First" already avoids this; keep it that way. Do not create marketing copy like "Proof First: Command of the Message for AI" — that phrasing implies endorsement/licensing.
- **State non-affiliation explicitly and prominently**, mirroring SimpleEnglish's closing line ("Unofficial project, not affiliated with or endorsed by ASD or STEMG. ASD-STE100 is a registered trademark of ASD.") — but do it for *three* rights-holders, each named individually with its own trademark-ownership sentence, not blended into one disclaimer. Put this in the README, in the SKILL.md, and in the LICENSE/NOTICE file, not just one of the three.
- **Reproduce zero training-deck structure or verbatim phrasing.** Paraphrase only the publicly documented, widely-taught *concept* (e.g., "articulate differentiated value against named alternatives," not Force Management's specific named sub-components if those are part of licensed training content rather than public description). Where public secondary sources (books, public blog posts by the framework's own creators, Wikipedia-level descriptions) exist, ground the paraphrase in those, and treat vendor training-portal content as off-limits regardless of how easy it is for a model to reproduce.
- **Treat the MEDDIC/MEDDICC/MEDDPICC family as the highest-risk of the three** given its documented history of enforcement against community content and its live inter-vendor dispute. Consider naming the concept generically ("a qualification-completeness checklist in the tradition of MEDDIC-style frameworks") with a single factual footnote on ownership/history, rather than asserting a specific spelling's status — and get this specific section reviewed by counsel before publishing, given the active litigation.
- **Do not claim certification, endorsement, or "official" status anywhere**, including implicitly (e.g., do not say "graded against Command of the Message" as if the rubric were licensed).
- **License:** MIT for the repo's own code/prose is fine and matches SimpleEnglish precedent; it says nothing about the trademark status of the referenced names, and the README/NOTICE must make that distinction clear (code license vs. trademark disclaimer are different legal instruments).

**Warning signs:**
- Any README/marketing copy that reads as "the AI version of Command of the Message" rather than "a skill informed by ideas associated with Command of the Message."
- A rule catalog section that, when compared side-by-side with a Force Management or MEDDIC training deck's public excerpts, shares structure, ordering, or near-identical phrasing rather than just the same high-level concept.
- No dated legal review of the trademark/disclaimer language before the repo goes public, given that one of the three families has documented, recent (2026) contested litigation.
- Marketing badges, shields.io badges, or taglines that use any of the three marks as if they were the project's own achievement badge (SimpleEnglish's badge pattern — "74.6% fewer violations" — is safe because it measures the *skill's own* rule set, not a certification claim against someone else's mark; a "MEDDICC-certified" badge would not be safe).

**Phase to address:**
Legal/disclaimer and public-repo-scaffolding phase — this should be an explicit phase or explicit phase gate, not a footnote task, given the litigation history found in this research. Recommend a dedicated review step before the public README/SKILL.md ships, distinct from and prior to the general "public repo scaffolding" requirement already listed in PROJECT.md.

### Pitfall 4: The benchmark becomes a self-serving instrument — the standard LLM-eval failure modes, applied to a project whose entire premise is "no claim without evidence"

**What goes wrong:**
PROJECT.md commits to a multi-model baseline-vs-skill benchmark and a blind pairwise judge, explicitly because "the repo cannot demand evidence it does not itself provide." That raises the stakes on every standard LLM-eval failure mode, because for this project a bad benchmark isn't just bad science — it's the specific hypocrisy the skill exists to prevent, and a hostile reader (a competent technical evaluator, exactly this project's target audience) will look for it first.

Concretely, the known failure modes and their mitigations, adapted to this project:

| Failure mode | How it shows up here | Mitigation | Honest reporting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Judge position bias** | A pairwise judge shown (baseline, skill) in a fixed order will systematically favor whichever slot it sees first/last; measured swings of 10-15 points of win-rate are reported in the literature. | Shuffle/counter-balance order on every pair, score both orders, average — SimpleEnglish already does this ("scored... twice with the texts in both orders"). Do the same here as a hard requirement, not optional. | State explicitly in RESULTS.md that both orders were scored and averaged, and show the raw per-order breakdown, not just the averaged number. |
| **Self-preference / family bias** | If the judge model is in the same family as the generator (e.g., a Claude judge scoring Claude-generated skill output), research finds self-preference effects on the order of 10-25%. This project's stated plan is multi-model (per PROJECT.md), which is good, but the judge itself is still a single model choice. | Use a judge from a different family than at least some of the generators being judged, or run the judge pass with two different judge families and report both. At minimum, state which family the judge belongs to relative to each generator, exactly as SimpleEnglish's RESULTS.md does ("The judge is a Claude model and the texts are Claude output, so family bias is possible"). | Do not average away the caveat — name it per-model in the results table, especially for any row where judge and generator share a family. |
| **Cherry-picked scenarios** | If the worked scenarios and benchmark tasks are all drawn from the one fictional cloud-migration deal (per PROJECT.md's decision to use a single deal for all examples), there is a real risk that the deal was authored, consciously or not, to be a favorable proving ground for the rule catalog — i.e., the tasks were picked because the skill is known to do well on them. | Author the benchmark scenario set and the rule catalog by different passes with a time gap, or explicitly stress-test with at least one scenario type the rule catalog was *not* designed around (e.g., a hostile/adversarial customer objection-handling scenario, not just a clean proposal-writing scenario). Document the scenario list publicly so outsiders can judge representativeness themselves. | Publish the full scenario set (as SimpleEnglish publishes `scenarios.json`/`pressure-tests.md`), and state plainly that all scenarios derive from one fictional deal, with the tradeoff (vendor-neutral, mutually reinforcing) named alongside the limitation (single-domain, may not generalize to other deal types/verticals). |
| **Unfair baseline (strawman prompt)** | If the "baseline" condition is a bare, under-specified prompt while the "skill" condition gets a well-engineered task description in addition to the rule catalog, the comparison measures "prompting help" not "rule catalog value." | Use the identical user-facing task prompt for both conditions, varying only the presence/absence of the skill file itself — this is exactly the Pi cross-check's stated method ("sent only the scenario prompt for the baseline condition... added the complete SKILL.md text for the skill condition... same neutral system prompt for both"). Adopt that method explicitly and state it in the methodology section. | State the exact baseline prompt and system prompt used, verbatim, in the results file — not just "we used a baseline." |
| **Linter tautology** (rules encode the skill's own vocabulary, so "improvement" is circular) | This is a materially bigger risk here than for SimpleEnglish. STE's linter counts objective mechanical features (sentence length, contractions, banned modals) that exist independent of the skill's authorship. This project's most important rule — the deletion test — is a semantic judgment about whether a term carries denotation, which cannot be regex-detected at all (see Pitfall 5), so the linter is forced to proxy it with something like "count of terms from a maintained buzzword-adjacent list" or "count of unsupported superlatives." If that proxy list is authored by the same person/process that writes the SKILL.md's example buzzwords, the benchmark literally rewards the skill for avoiding a list it was told to avoid — a tautology, not a measurement. | Build the linter's proxy word list independently of the SKILL.md's own worked examples (e.g., derive it from an external corpus of real RFP/marketing language, not from the rule catalog's own illustrative "synergy, best-of-breed" examples), and keep the two artifacts (rule catalog examples vs. linter word list) maintained separately with different provenance, exactly as SimpleEnglish separates the copyrighted-and-not-reproduced ASD dictionary from its own regex list. | Name this limitation explicitly in the linter's own docstring and in RESULTS.md: "the buzzword-proxy word list is illustrative, not exhaustive or independently validated; a term absent from the list is not certified compliant, and the deletion test itself is not machine-checkable" — do not let the number imply more rigor than a regex list can offer. |
| **Insufficient sample size / unreported variance** | SimpleEnglish's own RESULTS.md admits "one generation per cell" and calls out that reasoning-effort levels moved its headline number from 85.0% to 90.2% on the same scenarios — i.e., a single run understates true variance and the headline number is effort-sensitive. This project, with a more subjective persuasion dimension layered on top of a linter dimension, will have even more run-to-run variance because "did this feel persuasive" is noisier than "is this sentence under 20 words." | Run at least 2-3 generations per model/scenario/condition cell where budget allows, and report a range or standard deviation, not a single point estimate — or explicitly adopt SimpleEnglish's honesty pattern of stating "one generation per cell, re-run for variance" as a caveat rather than silently presenting a single run as definitive. Pin and report the reasoning-effort/thinking-budget setting per model, since it visibly moves the number. | Publish raw per-generation files (as SimpleEnglish does under `results/raw/*.json`) so any reader can recompute variance themselves — this is the single highest-leverage honesty move available and costs nothing once the harness exists. |

**Why it happens:**
All of these are documented, measured phenomena in the LLM-as-judge and instruction-following literature (position bias measured at 10-15 points of win-rate swing; self-preference measured at 10-25%; the general finding that judges without explicit debiasing produce inflated, order-sensitive, family-biased scores). They are not exotic — they are the default behavior of naive eval harnesses, and SimpleEnglish's own RESULTS.md shows they are already only partially addressed even in the reference project (it flags family bias as a known open risk it did not resolve, only disclosed).

**How to avoid:**
Adopt every mitigation in the table above as a written methodology requirement before the first benchmark run, not as a post-hoc fix. Specifically: counter-balanced judge order (mandatory), judge-family disclosure per row (mandatory), identical baseline/skill task prompts (mandatory), an independently-sourced linter proxy list (mandatory given the tautology risk is structurally worse here than in the reference project), and either multi-run variance reporting or an explicit single-run caveat (minimum bar). Publish the full scenario set and raw generations.

**Warning signs:**
- The RESULTS.md draft has a single number ("X% fewer violations") with no caveats section at all.
- The judge is the same model family as every generator and the results file doesn't mention it.
- The buzzword/proxy word list used by the linter is identical to, or trivially derived from, the SKILL.md's own worked "avoid these words" examples.
- Scenarios were written by the same person, same week, as the rule catalog, with no adversarial/out-of-distribution scenario included.

**Phase to address:**
Eval/benchmark harness phase, as its own gated phase with an explicit "honest caveats" deliverable (mirroring RESULTS.md's "Honest number warnings" section), reviewed before any headline number is published in the README.

### Pitfall 5: The linter's regex-vs-semantic gap is worse here than in the reference project, and copying the reference's caveat language would understate it

**What goes wrong:**
SimpleEnglish's linter docstring is admirably honest about its own limits: "this is a regex pass, not a grammar parser... it undercounts... numbers from this tool are comparable between two texts run through the same version; they are not a compliance verdict." That honesty works for STE because most of STE's rules — sentence length, banned modals, contractions, perfect tense, semicolons — are genuinely close to regex-detectable; the gap between the linter and the standard is a matter of degree (some passive voice missed, some markdown edge cases), not a matter of kind.

This project's governing rule — the deletion test — is different in *kind*, not degree. Whether a term "changes the technical meaning" when deleted requires a semantic judgment about denotation vs. connotation that no regex can make. A linter here can only count *symptoms correlated with* buzzword use (unquantified superlatives, claims with no adjacent number, hedge words, sentence length, passive voice) — none of which *is* the deletion test. If the published caveat language is copied verbatim from SimpleEnglish ("it undercounts, it is not a compliance verdict") without being rewritten to say something stronger — that the linter does not, and structurally cannot, check the rule the skill is actually built around — a careful reader (again: this project's own target audience, technical evaluators) will notice the mismatch between the boldness of the headline number and the honesty of the caveat, and conclude the project is quietly overstating what its own measurement proves. That is the exact failure mode Pitfall 4's "linter tautology" row describes, viewed from the disclosure side rather than the measurement-design side.

**Why it happens:**
It's tempting to reuse working disclaimer language wholesale because it already exists, is proven, and sounds appropriately humble — but disclaimer language is not portable between rule systems with different formal properties. STE's rules are mostly syntactic (countable); this project's central rule is semantic (judged). Reusing the caveat without reassessing its accuracy is a documentation shortcut that produces a false impression of comparable rigor.

**How to avoid:**
- Write the linter's docstring and RESULTS.md caveats to state, explicitly and specifically, that the **deletion test itself is not machine-checkable** and that the linter's buzzword-adjacent counts (unquantified superlatives, claims without adjacent evidence, hedges) are *correlates* selected by the project authors, not an implementation of the rule — a stronger and more specific claim than SimpleEnglish's "it undercounts."
- Report the linter's proxy metrics and the persuasion-judge score as two named, separately-labeled numbers, never blended into one headline figure — so a reader can see "violations-per-100-words (mechanical proxy)" is a different, weaker claim than "judge-rated persuasiveness/evidence density (semantic)."
- Consider adding one additional evaluation layer the reference project doesn't have: a small held-out set of hand-labeled deletion-test judgments (human or careful separate-model annotation) that the linter's proxy count is validated against, with the correlation (or lack of it) reported honestly. This is optional but would be the single strongest piece of evidence that the numbers mean what they claim.
- In the SKILL.md's own check-mode instructions, make explicit that check mode's semantic judgment (applying the deletion test to actual text) is the authoritative check, and the linter is a benchmarking convenience only — never let a user believe passing the linter is equivalent to passing the deletion test.

**Warning signs:**
- The linter docstring is a near-verbatim copy of SimpleEnglish's wording with only nouns swapped.
- RESULTS.md reports one aggregate "violations-per-100-words" number without separating mechanical-proxy counts from judge-rated persuasion/evidence scores.
- No worked example exists where the linter says "clean" but a human applying the actual deletion test would flag a term (or vice versa) — meaning the gap was never empirically demonstrated, only asserted.

**Phase to address:**
Linter design phase and eval/benchmark harness phase (the caveat language should be drafted alongside the linter itself, not retrofitted after a headline number already exists in a README draft).

### Pitfall 6: The skill fails to fire, gets partially ignored, or invents its own rule numbers — the same failure classes SimpleEnglish had to design against, now compounded by a longer, three-framework rule catalog

**What goes wrong:**
Several distinct, well-documented skill/agent failure modes apply directly:
- **Description-field non-triggering.** Claude (and other harnesses) select skills primarily off the YAML `description` field, and the documented failure pattern is a description written as a summary ("this skill helps with presales writing") rather than a trigger ("use when writing RFP responses, solution proposals, executive summaries..."). With three anchor frameworks and a broader artifact scope (RFP/RFI, proposals, executive summaries, demo/discovery material) than SimpleEnglish's single "technical documentation" trigger surface, the description has more ways to be too narrow (misses a valid use) or too vague (fails to specifically out-compete generic "help me write" requests) in the harness's skill-selection ranking.
- **Length vs. attention.** Instruction-following research shows perfect-response rates collapsing as instruction/constraint count rises, with near-zero full compliance once instruction length or constraint count crosses documented thresholds (studies report degradation setting in well before instruction counts as low as 80, and near-zero success once instructions exceed several thousand words). This project's rule catalog is structurally larger than SimpleEnglish's single-standard, single-mode catalog: three frameworks (rule spine + completeness audit + opening rule), plus integrity/anti-fabrication rules, plus per-artifact patterns for at least four document types, plus write mode and check mode. If all of this lives in one SKILL.md loaded in full every time, the realistic risk is that later sections (per-artifact patterns, the self-check) get followed less reliably than the first rules the model reads — exactly the "lost in the middle" pattern documented in long-instruction research.
- **Rules that conflict with model defaults.** Models default toward hedged, superlative-friendly, safety-hedged prose (their RLHF training rewards caution and comprehensiveness) — the deletion test and the integrity/anti-fabrication rules fight that default directly, which is good, but "must" and "must not" language competes with a model's trained instinct to hedge ("it's worth noting that," "in some cases"), and instructions phrased as "should" are documented to be read by models as optional/soft, not mandatory — SimpleEnglish explicitly bans "should" for exactly this reason ("models read 'should' as optional"). Every rule in this project's catalog needs the same audit: is any governing instruction phrased with "should," a trailing condition, or a soft qualifier that a model will silently deprioritize?
- **Hallucinated rule numbers in check mode.** SimpleEnglish's own recorded baseline failure (pre-skill, asked to write STE from memory) invented plausible-sounding rule citations ("Rule 3.1: short sentences" when the real 3.1 is verb forms). This project has *three separate numbering/reference systems* to keep straight (Command of the Message elements, MEDDICC checklist items, the rule catalog's own numbered rules, plus Challenger's single opening rule) — more surface area for a model in check mode to confidently cite "MEDDICC criterion 4" or "Rule 12" that doesn't correspond to anything actually in the file.

**Why it happens:**
Skill-selection ranking, instruction-length attention decay, and default-hedging behavior are documented, measured properties of how current LLMs process system/skill context — they are not implementation bugs to be patched once, they are ongoing constraints the design has to work within and re-test as models change (see Pitfall 8).

**How to avoid:**
- Write the `description` field as an explicit trigger list naming every artifact type and every likely user phrasing ("RFP response," "RFI," "solution proposal," "executive summary," "demo script," "discovery document," "presales," "sales engineering," plus framework names a user might invoke — "MEDDICC," "Command of the Message," "Challenger"), following the documented pattern of "what + when," keyword-rich, single consistent viewpoint, front-loaded before any length truncation point.
- Split the rule catalog the way SimpleEnglish splits its own: a lean SKILL.md carrying the core rule spine, integrity rules, and the mandatory self-check, with per-artifact patterns and the MEDDICC completeness audit pushed into separate `references/*.md` files loaded on demand — this is explicitly the structure PROJECT.md already anticipates ("`SKILL.md` (rule catalog, two modes, self-check), plus three references") and should be treated as a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have, given the documented attention-decay risk of one long file.
- Audit every rule statement for "should," "would," "might," trailing "if"/"when" conditions, and any construction that reads as optional, exactly as SimpleEnglish's own Section 3 modal rule does — apply the audit to this project's own SKILL.md as a meta-rule, not only to the *output* the skill produces.
- Design check mode the same way SimpleEnglish does: "cite only rule numbers/checklist items that exist in this file, not from memory," paired with an actual pressure-test scenario (ask a fresh baseline agent, with no skill loaded, to recite the frameworks' components from memory) recorded before the skill ships, so the specific failure the skill must close is documented, not assumed. Given three frameworks instead of one, run this baseline test three times — once per framework — since a model might hallucinate confidently on one framework's numbering and correctly recall another's.

**Warning signs:**
- A fresh test session with the skill installed does not autonomously apply it when given a natural, unprompted request like "help me write a response to this RFP question" (only fires when the user explicitly names the skill or a framework).
- In check mode, a baseline (no-skill) run confidently cites a MEDDICC letter or a Command-of-the-Message element that doesn't match this project's own definitions.
- Any rule in the SKILL.md contains "should," "it is recommended that," or a condition trailing at the end of a long sentence.
- The self-check step (mandatory in SimpleEnglish's design) is missing, optional-sounding, or buried past the point where attention has already decayed in a long file.

**Phase to address:**
SKILL.md authoring/structure phase (description field, file-splitting, modal-verb audit) and the pressure-test phase (recorded baseline failures per framework, re-tested after each revision — mirroring SimpleEnglish's `pressure-tests.md` methodology exactly).

### Pitfall 7: Domain-specific presales hazards the skill must refuse or flag, not just write around

**What goes wrong:**
Presales writing has professional/legal hazards that are specific to the genre and not covered by "don't fabricate" alone:
- **Commitments that become contractual.** In RFP responses and proposals, "will" is not stylistic — legal analysis of RFP language treats "will" as capable of creating a binding contractual obligation when the proposal is incorporated into a contract or SOW, on the same footing as "shall" in many procurement contexts. A skill whose rule catalog bans hedged language ("should," "might") and pushes writers toward confident, active-voice, "will"-style commitments — which is exactly the right move for persuasion and clarity — can, without a corresponding safeguard, cause the writer to accidentally commit the vendor to specific dates, SLAs, or deliverables that legal/delivery teams never agreed to.
- **Disclosing another customer as a reference without authorization.** Presales documents routinely want to cite "a similar customer" or a named reference account for social proof; an integrity-focused skill that pushes for concrete, named evidence (correctly, per Pitfall 4/5) creates pressure to name a real customer, use case, or metric that the writer may not be authorized to disclose (NDA, reference-approval process, anonymization requirements).
- **Competitor comparisons that create legal exposure.** Under the Lanham Act (and equivalent regimes elsewhere), comparative claims about a named competitor must be true, non-misleading, and — critically — backed by evidence; even literally true comparisons can be actionable if presented misleadingly, and there is a legal presumption of deception for deliberately disseminated, literally false comparative claims, with remedies including injunctions and treble damages. "Puffery" (vague, unfalsifiable superlative claims about your own product) is generally protected, but specific, falsifiable claims about a *named competitor* are not — which inverts this project's own rule logic: the deletion test wants to eliminate vague puffery in favor of specific claims, and specific claims about a competitor are exactly the ones that carry the most legal risk if unverified.
- **Export/compliance language.** Cloud/technical presales content (the project's own worked-example domain) routinely touches data residency, export-control-sensitive technology categories, and compliance claims (SOC2, FedRAMP, ITAR/EAR-adjacent language) that must not be asserted unless the writer has verified certification status — this is a special case of "no claim without evidence" but with a materially higher cost of being wrong (regulatory, not just reputational).

**Why it happens:**
The skill's core discipline — replace vague adjectives with specific, evidenced claims — is exactly right for making prose persuasive and exactly the mechanism that turns a soft, deniable statement into a hard, checkable, and potentially legally consequential one. Specificity is good for the reader's trust and dangerous for the writer's legal exposure if the specificity is either unauthorized (someone else's data) or unverified (a competitor claim, a compliance claim, a delivery date). This is a direct, structural side effect of the project's own central design choice (evidence over adjectives), not an unrelated bolt-on concern.

**How to avoid:**
- Extend the Integrity section (already planned per PROJECT.md) beyond "do not fabricate metrics/references/benchmarks/certifications" to explicitly flag, not silently write, four categories: (1) forward-looking commitments phrased with "will"/imperative in a document type that could be contractually incorporated — the skill should surface a flag like "this reads as a commitment; confirm with delivery/legal before sending" rather than deciding for the user; (2) any named-customer reference or case-study detail — require the user to confirm disclosure authorization exists, and default to anonymized/generalized framing when unconfirmed; (3) any comparative claim naming a specific competitor — require the claim to be evidence-backed per the same "no claim without evidence" rule, and flag it for legal/marketing review before external use, since competitor claims carry categorically higher liability than self-claims; (4) compliance/certification/export-control language — treat these as a strict subset of the anti-fabrication rule with zero tolerance, since the downstream cost (regulatory exposure) is categorically higher than a reputational hit.
- Make these four categories part of the rule catalog's numbered structure (not a vague "be careful" footnote) so check mode can cite them exactly like any other rule, and so the linter/self-check can search for their textual signatures (named competitor + comparative adjective; "will" + a date/number; a named customer/company string; certification acronyms) the same way SimpleEnglish's self-check searches for contractions and modals.
- Explicitly state in the skill's Limits section (mirroring SimpleEnglish's own "Limits" section) that the skill flags these categories but does not and cannot verify authorization, accuracy, or legal risk — this is a "the skill refuses to invent facts... but cannot fact-check what it is given" boundary PROJECT.md already states for facts generally; this pitfall just names the four specific, professionally consequential subclasses that need their own explicit callout rather than being left implicit in the general rule.

**Warning signs:**
- Worked examples on the fictional cloud-migration deal contain unflagged "will" commitments with specific dates/SLAs, unflagged named-competitor comparisons, or compliance claims with no evidence marker.
- The Integrity section's language only discusses fabrication ("don't invent a number") and never discusses authorization/disclosure or legal-review flags for true-but-risky content.
- Check mode has no rule number a user could cite for "flag this competitor comparison for legal review."

**Phase to address:**
Integrity/rule-catalog phase (these four categories belong in the same catalog section as the anti-fabrication rules, authored together) and per-artifact patterns phase (RFP/proposal patterns specifically need the commitment-language flag; demo/discovery patterns need the competitor-comparison flag).

### Pitfall 8: Public agent-skill repos decay after launch — spec drift, harness changes, and stale benchmarks compound faster here than for a single-standard skill

**What goes wrong:**
Four decay vectors, each concretely worse for this project than for a single-anchor project like SimpleEnglish:
- **Agent Skills standard drift.** The project commits to Agent Skills compatibility across Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Copilot, Gemini CLI, OpenCode, "and the rest" (per PROJECT.md). The standard is young and actively evolving (field length limits, truncation behavior, and description-parsing conventions have already changed during the standard's life, per current authoring guidance). A repo with three reference files and a longer rule catalog has more surface area to break when field-length limits or frontmatter parsing rules change than a minimal single-file skill.
- **Harness changes breaking distribution.** SimpleEnglish ships via multiple channels (skills CLI, Claude Code plugin marketplace, output style, paste-able system prompt) — each channel is a distinct integration point that can silently break on a harness update (a plugin marketplace schema change, an output-style API change) without the skill's own content changing at all. This project inherits the same multi-channel surface by design (PROJECT.md: "distributed the same way SimpleEnglish is").
- **Benchmark results going stale as models change.** The published headline number is model- and version-specific by construction (SimpleEnglish's own table names specific dated model versions). New model releases can shift baseline behavior in either direction — a new model might already write closer to the desired style unprompted (shrinking the skill's measured lift and making the headline number look inflated/stale), or might regress on some dimension the old benchmark didn't cover. Because this project adds a *judged* persuasion dimension on top of a linted mechanical dimension, it has twice the surface area that can go stale, and the persuasion judge score is more sensitive to judge-model version drift than a regex linter score is.
- **Unmaintained vocabulary/proxy lists.** Even though the deletion test itself needs no maintained blocklist (a deliberate design win per PROJECT.md), the *linter's* buzzword-proxy list, the per-artifact pattern examples, and the fictional deal's technical details (cloud service names, pricing-adjacent details) are all maintained artifacts that will drift out of date as cloud vendor terminology, competitor names, and market buzzwords evolve — "next-generation" gets replaced by whatever 2027's version of that word is, and a static example list ages the same way any "current buzzwords" list does.

**Why it happens:**
Open-source infra/tooling projects reliably decay when their maintenance burden exceeds the maintainer's ongoing attention, and multi-framework, multi-channel, benchmark-carrying projects have a structurally higher maintenance burden per unit of adoption than single-file, single-claim projects — the same properties that make this project rigorous (three frameworks, multiple distribution channels, a full eval harness) are the properties that make it expensive to keep current.

**How to avoid:**
- Version-pin every benchmark claim to specific, dated model identifiers (as SimpleEnglish already does) and add a visible "last verified" date to the README/RESULTS.md, with a stated policy (even informal) for how often the benchmark is expected to be re-run — a stale, undated headline number is worse for credibility than no number.
- Treat the plugin-marketplace manifest, output-style definition, and system-prompt export as generated artifacts from a single source of truth (the SKILL.md and references), not hand-maintained duplicates, so a standard/schema change requires updating generation logic once rather than four files independently drifting out of sync.
- Keep the linter's proxy word list and the fictional deal's technical/vocabulary details in their own separately-versioned file(s) with a lightweight "last reviewed" marker, so vocabulary staleness is visible and fixable without touching the rule catalog itself.
- Explicitly scope what "maintained" means for v1 (e.g., "benchmark re-run each time a named model family ships a new flagship version" or an even lighter commitment) rather than implying an open-ended maintenance promise the project can't sustain — an honest, modest maintenance commitment is more credible long-term than an implicit unlimited one.

**Warning signs:**
- README benchmark numbers have no date and no model-version pin.
- The plugin marketplace manifest, output style, and system-prompt files have all diverged from the SKILL.md content (checkable by diffing content, not just checking they all "exist").
- The fictional deal's cloud terminology/vocabulary examples read as dated (referencing service names or buzzwords a reader would recognize as several years stale) with no update since initial publication.

**Phase to address:**
Public-repo-scaffolding phase (single-source-of-truth generation for multi-channel artifacts) and post-launch/maintenance planning (explicit, modest re-benchmark cadence stated publicly) — this is a phase-boundary concern for the roadmap's final phase and for any post-v1 milestone planning.

## Technical Debt Patterns

| Shortcut | Immediate Benefit | Long-term Cost | When Acceptable |
|----------|-------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Copy SimpleEnglish's linter/caveat wording verbatim, swap nouns | Fast to ship, proven wording | Understates this project's harder regex-vs-semantic gap (Pitfall 5); looks evasive once noticed | Never as final wording — fine as a first draft to then materially strengthen |
| Blend Command of the Message, MEDDICC, and Challenger into one flat rule list instead of three distinct catalogs | Simpler SKILL.md, less to design | Produces the "category error" PROJECT.md itself warns against (qualification framework governing sentences); breaks check-mode citation clarity | Never — PROJECT.md has already ruled this out as a settled decision |
| Single-run benchmark ("one generation per cell") for v1 | Ships the eval harness faster, matches SimpleEnglish precedent exactly | Understates variance; a single lucky/unlucky run becomes the permanent headline number | Acceptable only if explicitly caveated per Pitfall 4, and only as a stated interim state with a plan to add repeat runs |
| Use one judge model, same family as generators, for the pairwise pass | Cheapest, fastest eval setup | Self-preference bias (10-25% documented) inflates the headline "skill wins" number in a way a sophisticated reader will discount or distrust | Acceptable only with explicit, prominent family-bias disclosure; better to budget for a cross-family judge before publishing |
| Defer the four Integrity flag categories (commitment language, reference disclosure, competitor claims, compliance/export) to "v2" | Ships the core rule catalog faster | These are the exact categories where the skill's own specificity-over-adjectives mechanism creates new legal exposure (Pitfall 7) — deferring them ships a skill that is more legally dangerous than a plain adjective-heavy writer would have been | Not acceptable for v1 given "Integrity as its own catalog section" is already a PROJECT.md requirement; these four are a natural extension of that section, not a separate future scope |

## Integration/Distribution Gotchas

| Channel | Common Mistake | Correct Approach |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Agent Skills standard (Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Copilot, Gemini CLI, OpenCode) | Assuming one harness's description-length/frontmatter behavior generalizes to all; the standard's truncation and parsing conventions have shifted during its short life | Test the description field's actual truncation point per target harness before finalizing wording; keep the highest-value trigger keywords first regardless of harness |
| Claude Code plugin marketplace | Treating the marketplace manifest as a one-time artifact instead of a generated view of the SKILL.md | Regenerate/validate the manifest whenever the SKILL.md or references change; do not hand-edit both independently |
| Output style / system-prompt export | Letting the paste-able system prompt drift from the full SKILL.md content as the rule catalog grows (this project's catalog is larger than SimpleEnglish's, so drift risk is higher) | Generate the system-prompt export from the same source file(s) as SKILL.md, or explicitly version and re-derive it each time the rule catalog changes |
| Fictional cloud-migration deal as shared example substrate | Letting per-artifact examples (RFP answers, exec summaries, demo scripts) silently diverge on the deal's "facts" (numbers, timeline, stack) over time as different sections are edited independently | Maintain the deal's canonical facts (metrics, timeline, stack, customer profile) in one reference file that every worked example cites, not re-invented per example |

## Security/Legal Mistakes

| Mistake | Risk | Prevention |
|---------|------|------------|
| Naming a trademarked methodology in the project name, tagline, or badge | Nominative-fair-use defense weakens when use exceeds "reasonably necessary to identify," and any phrasing suggesting endorsement removes the defense's third prong entirely | Keep the project name and all badges free of the three marks; reference them only in body text discussing the concepts, with disclaimers (Pitfall 3) |
| Treating "MEDDPICC ruled generic" as covering "MEDDIC"/"MEDDICC" too | The April 2026 ruling (E.D. Pa., reported by MEDDICC Ltd, IPWatchdog, PR Newswire) addressed one spelling/registrant (01 Consulting LLC's "MEDDPICC"); MEDDICC Ltd's own marks and the wider family's status were not established as fully resolved by this research and should not be assumed safe by extension | Get explicit, current legal confirmation of each specific spelling/mark's status before publishing; do not rely on this research's summary as a legal conclusion |
| Letting the skill write specific, falsifiable competitor comparisons without an evidence/flag gate | Lanham Act exposure (false advertising, trade libel) attaches to specific, unverified, disseminated competitor claims, not to vague self-praise ("puffery"), which is protected | Require the same "no claim without evidence" rule for competitor claims as for self-claims, plus an explicit flag-for-review step (Pitfall 7) |
| Letting the skill write unverified compliance/certification/export-control claims because they make the document more persuasive | Regulatory exposure (false certification claims, export-control missteps) is categorically higher cost than a reputational miss | Zero-tolerance subset of the Integrity section; treat as always-flag, never-assert-without-explicit-user-confirmed-evidence |

## UX Pitfalls

| Pitfall | User Impact | Better Approach |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Check mode reports violations with no compliant rewrite, or a rewrite that is itself flat/lifeless (Pitfall 1 applied to check mode) | User loses trust that "compliant" and "good" are the same thing; may start ignoring the skill's rewrites | Every check-mode rewrite example must pass the same persuasion bar as write mode, not just the linter/deletion-test bar |
| Rule catalog structured so three frameworks feel like three unrelated appendices rather than one coherent authoring flow | User doesn't know which framework governs which decision at the point of writing (e.g., "am I checking completeness or articulation right now?") | Make the artifact-type patterns (RFP, proposal, exec summary, demo) the primary navigation, with each pattern explicitly stating which framework element applies at which point, rather than making the three frameworks the primary navigation |
| Integrity/flag categories (Pitfall 7) implemented as silent auto-edits (skill quietly removes a competitor name or a date) instead of visible flags | User doesn't learn why something changed, may re-add the risky content elsewhere without realizing why it was removed the first time | Always surface flags as visible, explained notes in the output ("flagged: this is a specific competitor claim — confirm evidence before sending"), never a silent deletion |

## "Looks Done But Isn't" Checklist

- [ ] **Rule catalog:** Often missing an explicit persuasion-preservation mechanism paired with each subtractive rule — verify every "delete X" rule has an adjacent "replace with concrete Y" instruction, not just a ban.
- [ ] **Deletion test:** Often missing the provenance override for customer/RFP-sourced terms — verify at least one pressure-test scenario supplies a mock RFP excerpt with the customer's own "buzzword" language and checks it is retained, not deleted.
- [ ] **Trademark disclaimers:** Often present but generic/blended — verify each of the three rights-holders gets its own named non-affiliation sentence, in the README, SKILL.md, and NOTICE/LICENSE area, not a single combined footnote.
- [ ] **Benchmark RESULTS.md:** Often reports a headline number without the position-bias, family-bias, sample-size, and linter-proxy-tautology caveats spelled out per-item — verify each of the five failure modes in Pitfall 4's table has an explicit sentence in the published caveats, not just a generic "results may vary."
- [ ] **Linter docstring:** Often copies "it undercounts, not a compliance verdict" language without stating the *stronger* claim this project needs — verify it explicitly states the deletion test is not machine-checkable at all, not merely "undercounted."
- [ ] **Check mode:** Often tested only on skill-authored examples where citations are trivially correct — verify a fresh, no-skill baseline run was actually recorded inventing a rule number or framework element (per framework), before claiming the skill closes that gap.
- [ ] **Integrity section:** Often covers fabrication only — verify it explicitly names and flags (not silently handles) commitment language, reference-customer disclosure, competitor comparisons, and compliance/export claims as four distinct sub-rules.
- [ ] **Multi-channel distribution:** Often has the plugin manifest/output-style/system-prompt export tested once at launch and never re-verified — verify there's a single generation source or a documented sync-check step before each release.

## Recovery Strategies

| Pitfall | Recovery Cost | Recovery Steps |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| Flat, lifeless output shipped as v1 (Pitfall 1) | MEDIUM | Add a persuasion rubric to the judge pass retroactively, re-run pairwise judging with the new dimension, and revise the rule catalog to pair every subtractive rule with a constructive one; re-publish worked examples |
| Deletion test edge cases discovered post-launch via user reports (Pitfall 2) | LOW | Document the edge case publicly (mirroring SimpleEnglish's community-audit pattern in issue #4), patch the rule with the provenance override, re-test the specific scenario, and note the fix in a changelog |
| Trademark cease-and-desist or takedown request received (Pitfall 3) | HIGH | Immediately comply with any specific takedown of infringing material while seeking legal counsel; do not assume the project can rely on nominative fair use unilaterally once challenged; be prepared to rename/genericize the affected section (as this project's own generic-fallback wording in Pitfall 3 anticipates) |
| Benchmark headline number found to be inflated/biased by an external reviewer (Pitfall 4) | HIGH | Publish a public correction with the corrected methodology and number (transparency is the only credible recovery for a project whose premise is "no claim without evidence"); re-run with the fixed methodology before any further promotion |
| Linter/deletion-test gap called out publicly as dishonest (Pitfall 5) | MEDIUM | Strengthen the docstring/caveat language immediately, add the human-validated-sample comparison if not already present, and publicly acknowledge the gap was understated |
| Skill found not to fire reliably in a major harness (Pitfall 6) | LOW-MEDIUM | Rewrite the description field with harness-specific testing, split the SKILL.md further if length is implicated, and add the failure to the pressure-test suite so it's caught before the next release |
| A shipped worked example is later found to contain an unflagged competitor claim, contractual commitment, or unverified compliance claim (Pitfall 7) | MEDIUM-HIGH | Remove/correct the specific example immediately, retroactively apply the four-category flag rule across all worked examples, and treat this as evidence the Integrity section needs to ship in v1, not be deferred |
| Benchmark/distribution goes stale after a model or harness update (Pitfall 8) | LOW | Re-run the benchmark against current model versions, re-date the README, and verify multi-channel artifacts against the current standard/schema before the next release |
